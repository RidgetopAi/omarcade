Fire one

★ The rally — the thing this game is actually made of

A Breakout rally is a handful of hits buried in a brick shower. A Volley rally is two sounds alternating, for a long time — Brian's best is 59 returns. So the climb is not a flourish here, it is most of what you hear. Play a long one and listen for two things: does it keep rising for as long as the rally lasts, and does the wall bounce stay underneath the paddle instead of fighting it?
The spacing tightens as it goes, because the ball speeds up — an evenly spaced version would sound like a scale rather than a rally, which is the reassuring lie worth avoiding.

A whole point, end to end

Serve → rally → the point landing. The question this answers that "fire one" cannot: does the point sound arrive, or does it disappear under the rally it ends?

Tuning

paddle hit

Carried over unchanged from Pixel Break, where you tuned it down from 420 so it would stop fighting the brick. It is the same event, and the one number here that has already survived your ear across several sessions.

★ the rally climb

The cap matters more here than it did in Breakout. A Breakout rally rarely reaches the ceiling; a 59-return rally sits at it for most of its length, so the cap is not an edge case — it is the sound of a good rally. The step starts gentler than Breakout's 0.036 for the same reason: at that rate the climb would be over by return 22 and the other 37 would sound identical.

wall bounce

The rule your Pixel Break tuning established: the most-heard sound gets the clear middle, everything else keeps out of its way. In a rally the wall is heard more than the paddle while saying less — the player already watched the ball hit it. So it wants to be lower and quieter. 165 Hz is where you moved the bomb to, which is the one pitch in this register already known to sit clear of 245.

serve

Two notes rising — the ball is in play. Above the rally's register so it reads as a beginning rather than as another return.

point won / lost

One gesture, inverted: won rises, lost falls. Written as a single sound with a direction rather than two, so tuning cannot let them drift apart until winning and losing stop being a matched pair.

match won / lost

Three notes — a major triad, climbing for a win and reversed for a loss. Longer and louder than a point because nothing follows it: there is no next rally for it to get in the way of, which is the constraint every other sound here is shaped by. The loss keeps the major third rather than going minor — this is an arcade cabinet, and the falling direction already carries the meaning.
