The three numbers

Grid size, zoom, and world scale are independent. Confusing them is why sizing feels slippery.

There are three different "how big is it" numbers in play, and they control completely different things. Nothing in the game reads a sprite's pixel count as a size.

NumberControlsSet whereAffects the game?
Grid size
48 × 30		 How much detail you can express. Your detail budget. The size dropdown Yes — detail and aspect ratio
Zoom
15 px/cell		 How big the grid looks while you edit. A magnifying glass. The zoom slider No — none at all
World scale
prop / gantry / car		 How big the sprite actually is in the game. The role dropdown — and really, the Rust Yes — this is the real size

The one sentence that resolves the confusion

A billboard drawn at 32×32 and a billboard drawn at 128×128 come out the same physical size on screen. The 128 version just has 16× as many pixels packed into that same space, so each one is smaller — which is what "smoother" means.

See it

The same shape, same world size, at three grid densities. Only the pixel density changes — the object does not get bigger.

Zoom, by contrast, changes nothing

Dragging the zoom slider is exactly like leaning closer to the monitor. The output bytes are identical. It exists so you can see what you are doing while placing individual cells — nothing more. If you zoom out and the sprite "looks worse", that is not the zoom: that is the sprite telling you the truth about how it reads at real size.

Which is what the Preview and Scale check panels are for. Preview shows the art at three sizes on your chosen background. Scale check shows it standing on the road next to the reference car, at the size the game will actually draw it. Judge your work in the scale check, not on the big editing grid. The grid always flatters.

Bit depth vs resolution

"8-bit" bundles several ideas that are technically separate.

When people say a game is "8-bit" they usually mean the whole look of that console generation. Technically the term only ever meant the CPU's word size. The two things that actually shape the art are separate dials:

What it meansHistorical example
Bit depth How many colours can exist. 4-bit = 16 colours, 8-bit = 256, 24-bit = 16.7 million. NES: 4 colours per tile, 25 on screen
Resolution How many pixels across the screen. NES 256×240, SNES 256×224

Omarcade has neither limit. Softbuffer hands the game a raw 32-bit pixel per screen pixel — full 24-bit colour, at whatever the window resolution is. No palette cap, no sprite count limit, no scanline budget, no tile memory.

So every constraint in the art is a choice, not a limit. That is worth knowing in both directions: nothing stops you going smoother, and nothing forces you to. Chunkiness in Pole Position is deliberate period character, not the engine's ceiling.

The one constraint worth adopting voluntarily: a shared, limited palette across the whole suite. Not for technical reasons — because it is what will make eight games look like one product rather than eight unrelated hobby projects. Constraint is what makes a suite read as a suite.

Picking a grid size

Detail budget per asset, and how it maps to feel.

Pick the grid from how much detail the object needs, never from how big it is in the world. A distant billboard and a full-screen boss can want the same grid.

GridFeelGood for
16×16Classic arcade, very chunky Space Invaders aliens as originally drawn, small icons, bullets
24×24 – 32×32NES-era readable Player ship, pickups, small props
48×30Detailed but still clearly pixel art The Pole Position car (current default)
64×64Modern pixel art — shading, glow A Space Invaders alien with a modern twist
96×64 – 128×96Rich, near-smooth Billboards with lettering, detailed structures
160×60Wide structure Full-span gantry

The retro-to-modern dial, stated as a rule

Retro silhouette + modern density

Keep the shape faithful — the crab, the squid, the octopus are iconic because of their outlines. Then author that outline at 4–8× the original pixel density and spend the extra pixels on shading, rim light, and animation. The silhouette says retro; the rendering says 2026. That is how Shovel Knight and Celeste read as both at once.

Per-game guidance

  • Pole Position — stay chunky. It is a 1982 arcade cabinet and the blockiness is the aesthetic. Clean, smooth signage will feel wrong in a way that is hard to name.
  • Space Invaders — this is the testbed for the modern twist. 64×64 aliens, real shading, subtle animation.

Aspect ratio is the other half

The grid also fixes the sprite's shape. A 48×30 grid is 1.6:1 wide no matter what world size it is drawn at — the game scales it to fit its role, it never stretches it to a different ratio. So a billboard wants a roughly square or 4:3 grid; a gantry wants something very wide. Get the ratio right first, then worry about density.

Why colours are recipes

The palette is not hex — it is instructions, and that is what makes theme-reactivity work.

Most pixel editors store a fixed hex per palette slot. This one stores a recipe: a base colour, something to mix toward, and how far.

shadow   = body.lerp(theme.darker_background, 0.45)
tyre_top = tyre.lerp(theme.foreground, 0.14)

That second line derives from another derived tone, not from a theme slot. That chaining is the point: when the desktop theme moves, every tone moves together and stays related. Freeze them as hex and the sprite looks right under Everforest and subtly wrong under every other theme, silently.

A recipe's base can name: a fixed colour (livery), a theme slot (theme.foreground), or another palette letter (@T). The lerp here is byte-for-byte the one in core/src/backend/mod.rs, so what you see is what the game draws.

The exception: livery

Fixed colours exist for things that must not follow the theme. A red car that turns green with the desktop stops being a recognisable object. Livery, team colours, and anything the player identifies by colour belong in the fixed list.

Magenta means broken

A cell that renders magenta is a recipe that could not resolve — usually a dependency cycle, or a name the palette no longer knows. It is deliberately loud, in the same spirit as Sprite::new panicking rather than leaving a hole nobody notices until it is in a screenshot.

Named palettes

Save a palette by name and reload it on the next asset. This is what keeps eight billboards looking like they belong to the same roadside instead of eight separate art passes. Build the palette once, deliberately, then draw against it.

Drawing technique

The handful of things that separate pixel art that reads from pixel art that mushes.

Silhouette first

Block the whole shape in one flat colour before any detail. If it does not read as the object in solid black at real size, no amount of shading will save it. This is the single highest-leverage habit — and it is why the Scale check panel matters more than the editing grid.

Fewer colours than you think

Three tones per material — shadow, base, light — carries almost anything. A fourth for a rim highlight on the hero object. More than that and the shape gets noisy and stops reading at distance. The current car palette does the whole vehicle in eleven tones total.

Dithering

A checkerboard of two colours reads as an intermediate shade at distance. It is the classic way to get a gradient with a small palette, and it is strongly period-correct for the era Pole Position lives in. Use it for sky gradients and large flat panels. Do not use it on small sprites — below about 32px it just reads as noise.

Light from one direction

Pick a light source — conventionally upper-left — and be ruthlessly consistent. Every top-and-left face lighter, every bottom-and-right face darker. Inconsistent lighting is the most common reason art looks amateur without the viewer being able to say why.

Avoid pure black outlines everywhere

A hard black outline on every edge flattens the sprite. Outline the outer silhouette if you need it to pop against a busy background, but let interior boundaries be handled by tone contrast instead.

Work at the size it will be seen

Zoom in to place cells, then zoom out constantly to check. The most common mistake is detail that is invisible at real size — hours spent on a cockpit that occupies four pixels in the game. The preview strip is there to catch exactly this.

Tools & keys

Everything the playground can do.

Drawing tools

ToolKeyWhat it does
PencilP Paint single cells. Drag to draw.
FillF Flood-fill a connected region of one colour. The big one for billboards and flat panels.
LineL Drag from start to end. Straight edges without hand-counting.
RectangleR Drag a box outline. Hold shift for a filled box.

Panels

  • Canvas — the editing grid. Flatters everything; do not judge here.
  • Preview — the sprite at 1×, 2×, 4× on a chosen background. Check dark tyres against road.
  • Scale check — the sprite on the road, at the size the game will draw it, next to the reference car. This is the panel that tells the truth.
  • Palette — recipes. Click paints, the gear edits.
  • Assets — saved sprites, grid and palette together, in this browser only. art.rs remains the real archive.
  • Livery & custom — fixed colours that must not follow the theme.
  • Theme — paste a colors.toml to preview the sprite under any Omarchy theme before committing.

Onion skin

Load a saved asset as a faint underlay to trace or match against. Use it to keep a second billboard consistent with the first, or to line up frame 2 of an animation with frame 1.
